To draw an omikuji fortune, pray at the main hall first, then make a small coin offering at the omikuji stand. At Senso-ji in Asakusa you shake a box until a numbered stick slides out, and take your slip from the drawer with that number. Read it, keep it if it is good, and tie it at the rack provided if it is bad.

Stand in front of Senso-ji's main hall and you will hear it before you see it: a steady rattle of metal boxes being shaken, then a pause, then a laugh or a groan. People crowd around rows of small wooden drawers, comparing numbers and squinting at their slips. A few steps away, wires are thick with white paper knots. That is omikuji, and anyone can join in.

## ① What is omikuji? Advice in a paper slip

![Wooden drawers of omikuji fortune slips](/images/tours/asakusa-night-photo-walk/story-4.webp)

**【A thousand years of drawing lots】** Omikuji are fortune slips offered at Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines. The best-known set, a series of 100 short verses, is traditionally credited to Ryogen (912-985), a Tendai monk also known as Ganzan Daishi, and is often called the Kannon Hyakusen, the "100 lots of Kannon". Historians link the method to older Chinese divination with bamboo sticks in a tube. Shrines later developed their own slips, after the Meiji government separated Shinto and Buddhism in 1868.

**【Not a prediction, a message】** Sources agree on one point: the advice written on the slip matters more than the rank. Think of it as a short message for the months ahead, not a fixed future.

## ② How to draw: five steps at Senso-ji

Methods differ from place to place. Senso-ji, Tokyo's oldest temple, uses the classic stick-and-drawer system, and it works on trust.

1. **Pray first.** Visit the main hall and pay your respects before drawing. Many guides suggest this so you receive the message with a calm mind.
2. **Make the offering.** Drop your coin into the box at the stand yourself. The amount is posted there; check the sign and carry small coins.
3. **Shake the box.** Pick up the metal canister, shake it gently a few times, then tip it upside down. A thin stick with a number on it slides out of a small hole.
4. **Find your drawer.** The number is usually written in kanji. Match the characters on your stick with those on the drawer fronts, then return the stick to the canister.
5. **Take one slip.** Open the drawer and take the top slip only. Visitors report that Senso-ji slips carry an English translation on the back.

**【At a shrine】** Shrines often use their own slips and their own way of handing them out, so the steps may look different from Senso-ji. Follow the sign at each place, or ask at the shrine office.

## ③ Reading your slip: from daikichi to kyo

Look for the large character near the top. Ranks vary by place, but this is the common order:

| Rank | Japanese | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daikichi | 大吉 | Great blessing |
| Chukichi | 中吉 | Middle blessing |
| Shokichi | 小吉 | Small blessing |
| Kichi | 吉 | Blessing (its place in the order varies) |
| Suekichi | 末吉 | Blessing to come later |
| Kyo | 凶 | Bad luck, a warning |
| Daikyo | 大凶 | Great bad luck |

**【The small print】** Below the rank are short lines on different parts of life. Common headings include your wish (negaigoto), the person you are waiting for (machibito), lost items, travel, health, study and love. Read these slowly; this is where the real advice is.

**【Why Senso-ji feels harsh】** Senso-ji has a reputation for bad fortunes. Guides say it keeps the traditional Kannon Hyakusen proportions, in which roughly three in ten slips are reportedly bad, while many other places use gentler sets. The temple has not published a count of its slips, so treat the figure as a popular explanation, not a statistic.

## ④ Got a bad one? Tie it, don't redraw

![Guests praying at Hanazono Shrine](/images/tours/goldengai-nightlife/gallery-3.webp)

**【Leave the bad luck behind】** Fold the slip into a thin strip and tie it to the rack or wires set up for this. One popular explanation is a pun: matsu means both "pine tree", where slips were once tied, and "to wait", so the bad luck waits there instead of following you.

**【Use the right place】** Tie it only where the temple or shrine asks you to. Tying slips to trees can damage them, which is why many places now provide racks instead.

**【Don't draw again straight away】** An omikuji is treated as advice, and redrawing to escape it is seen as ignoring that advice. Senso-ji's own teaching, as guides describe it, is that what you do next can change your luck. Read the warning, then try again another day if you like.

## ⑤ Got a good one? Keep it close

- **Carry it.** Many people fold a good fortune into a wallet or bag as a small charm.
- **Or leave it.** Tying a good fortune at the rack is also acceptable.
- **Return it later.** When you are finished with a slip, bring it back to a temple or shrine rather than putting it in the trash.

## ⑥ Small manners that make a big difference

![Visitors walking toward the lit-up Hozomon gate](/images/tours/asakusa-night-photo-walk/gallery-2.webp)

- **Draw once.** One slip per visit is the custom.
- **Close the drawer** and put the stick back in the canister for the next person.
- **Step aside to read.** The stands get crowded, especially on weekends and at New Year.
- **Check the hours.** Omikuji stands and shrine offices keep their own hours, and some close in the evening. If you plan to draw late, check the official site of the temple or shrine first.

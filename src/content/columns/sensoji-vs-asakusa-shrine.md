Asakusa is a highlight of any Tokyo itinerary. While the streets are shoulder-to-shoulder with crowds during the day, the neighborhood reveals a completely different face after sunset. Walking through the illuminated grounds of Senso-ji Temple—after passing under the giant lantern of Kaminarimon glowing in the dark—offers a rare chance to quietly monopolize Japan's traditional beauty.

However, hidden within this stunning nighttime scenery is a mystery that puzzles many international travelers: "Why is a Shinto shrine (Asakusa Shrine) built right next to a massive Buddhist temple (Senso-ji)?"

## Where Buddhism Meets Shinto: Understanding Japan’s "Shinbutsu-Shugo"

![](/images/columns/sensoji-vs-asakusa-shrine/01.webp)

As you walk through the grounds of Senso-ji, you will spot the Torii gate of Asakusa Shrine immediately to your right. Seeing a shrine on temple grounds can seem incredibly strange if you are unfamiliar with Japan's historical background.

Temples are Buddhist facilities, a religion transmitted from India via China, dedicated to honoring Buddha. Shrines, on the other hand, are facilities of Shinto, Japan's indigenous nature-worship religion, dedicated to millions of deities (Kami). Although originally two completely different religions, Japan fostered a unique culture called "Shinbutsu-Shugo" (the syncretism of Shinto and Buddhism), worshiping them together for over 1,000 years.

The side-by-side existence of Senso-ji Temple and Asakusa Shrine is living proof of this history. They were cherished by the people as a single place of worship until the Meiji government issued an order to separate Shinto and Buddhism in the late 19th century.

## The True Meaning of the Five-Storied Pagoda Glowing in the Dark

![](/images/columns/sensoji-vs-asakusa-shrine/02.webp)

The most eye-catching structure within the Senso-ji grounds is the illuminated Five-Storied Pagoda. It is a perfect photo spot where the vermilion color pops against the pitch-black night sky, but do you know why this tower was built?

The pagoda is neither a mere decoration nor a watchtower. It originates from a "stupa" (a tomb) built to house the ashes of Buddha, the founder of Buddhism. It is constructed of five elements representing the Buddhist view of the universe from bottom to top: Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, and Space. The tower itself is a sacred object of prayer.

## How to Navigate a Shrine: The Meanings Behind the Torii Gate and Komainu

![](/images/columns/sensoji-vs-asakusa-shrine/03.webp)

When stepping from Senso-ji Temple into Asakusa Shrine, you must pass through a "Torii Gate." This gate acts as a boundary separating the secular world where humans live from the sacred realm of the deities. Bowing before passing through a Torii gate carries the same meaning as greeting someone when visiting their home.

Waiting for worshippers beyond the gate is a pair of stone statues called "Komainu" (guardian dogs). They act as the deities' protectors and ward off evil. If you look closely, the Komainu on the right has its mouth open ("A"), while the one on the left has its mouth closed ("Un"). This is called "A-Un breathing," which is said to represent the beginning and end of the universe.

> **💡 Family Highlight / One-Point Guide: Differences in Worshipping Styles** The way you pray differs between temples and shrines. The biggest difference is "clapping."

- **Temple (Senso-ji)**: Quietly press your hands together in front of your chest. Do not make a sound.
- **Shrine (Asakusa Shrine)**: "Two bows, two claps, one bow." Bow deeply twice, clap your hands loudly twice before praying, and then bow deeply one last time.

Be sure to put this difference into practice on your next trip to Japan!

Japanese history gets more fascinating the deeper you dig. Rather than simply tracing information from a guidebook, taking a quiet night walk to unravel these historical "whys" will make your travel memories richer and satisfy your intellectual curiosity.

Japanese restaurants display plastic food replicas, called shokuhin sampuru, so you can see exactly what a dish looks like, how big it is and what comes with it before you sit down. The best place in Tokyo to buy one, or to make your own, is Kappabashi, the kitchen-supply street between Ueno and Asakusa, where several specialist shops make replicas for restaurants and sell small versions to visitors.

Walk down Kappabashi on a weekday and the street feels like a backstage area for Tokyo's restaurants. Stacks of bowls spill out onto the pavement, knife shops glitter behind glass, and every few doors a window is full of ramen with chopsticks frozen mid-lift, glossy sushi and beer with a perfect head of foam that will never go flat.

Here is why those dishes exist, how to tell a good one from a cheap one, and where to shop.

## ① Why plastic food? Because a picture is not enough

![A tableware shop full of plates and bowls](/images/tours/asakusa-kappabashi-knife-tour/gallery-7.webp)

**【What the window tells you】**

A replica shows the real portion, the colours and the side dishes, so there is no guessing. A photo on a menu can flatter a dish. A life-size model on a shelf at eye level is much harder to exaggerate.

**【Why it helps travellers】**

Many small restaurants have menus only in Japanese. With a window display, you can simply point at the dish you want, or take a photo of it and show it to the staff.

**【Why not real food?】**

Real food on display spoils quickly. A well-made replica keeps its look for years, which is why restaurants are willing to pay for them even though a single model can cost far more than the dish it copies.

## ② Wax, an omelette and a disputed origin story

The invention is usually credited to Takizo Iwasaki, who is said to have made a wax model of omurice (omelette over rice) in 1932. He went on to found the company now called Iwasaki Mokei Seizo in his hometown, Gujo Hachiman in Gifu Prefecture, and it is widely described as Japan's largest maker of food samples.

The story is not as tidy as it sounds. Other accounts point to earlier models in Kyoto around 1917, and department store cafeterias in the 1920s already used simple food models to show their dishes. What most sources agree on is the timing: as Western-style dishes spread in Japan in the early 20th century, restaurants needed a way to show customers food they had never seen.

**【From wax to plastic】**

Early models were made of wax, which softened in heat and faded in sunlight. From around the 1970s, makers moved to more durable plastic. Moulds are often taken from real food, and the colour is then added by hand, which is why the best pieces look almost edible.

## ③ Kappabashi: Tokyo's kitchen town and its sample shops

![The golden Kappa statue in Kappabashi](/images/tours/asakusa-kappabashi-knife-tour/story-1.webp)

Kappabashi Dougu Street runs for nearly 800 metres north to south, with roughly 170 shops selling dishes, lacquerware, knives, pots, signs, uniforms and food packaging. Its local association traces the street back to around 1912, when dealers began selling used tools and hardware here. Most shops serve restaurant owners, but visitors are welcome.

Look for the golden Kappa statue and the giant chef's head on the Niimi building, two landmarks that help you get your bearings.

**【Sample shops to look for】**

- **Ganso Shokuhin Sample-ya**: its name means "the original food sample shop". It sells replica dishes and souvenirs and runs a hands-on workshop.
- **Maizuru**: a long-running maker that supplies restaurants and sells souvenir items such as key rings and phone cases.
- **Tokyo Biken**: another specialist known for magnets and phone accessories alongside full-size restaurant models.

Shop names and stock change, so treat this as a starting point and wander. Some shops do not allow photos inside, so ask first.

## ④ What to buy: from key rings to a full bowl of ramen

**【Small and easy to pack】**

- Key rings and magnets shaped like sushi, tempura or dumplings
- Phone accessories and small stationery items
- Single pieces of sushi or fruit

**【Bigger and more serious】**

- Full-size dishes like the ones in restaurant windows
- Custom orders, which the shops make for restaurants and take longer

Look closely before you buy. On good pieces the edges of fried batter are uneven, the rice grains are separate and the sauce has a slight shine. Prices range widely depending on size and how much handwork goes in, so check the tags. Wrap your pieces in clothing in your bag: thin parts like noodles and lettuce leaves can bend or snap.

## ⑤ Make your own: the tempura and lettuce workshop

At Ganso Shokuhin Sample-ya (3-7-6 Nishi-Asakusa), you can make your own replicas in a class of about 40 minutes. You pour coloured wax into warm water to form a lettuce leaf, then shape a piece of tempura, and take both home.

**【Before you go】**

- The class is taught in Japanese, but the steps are shown by hand, so it is easy to follow.
- Booking ahead is recommended. Some guides say you can join on the day if there is space, others say reservations are required.
- Times, the fee and the minimum age can change. Check the official site before you plan your day.

## ⑥ When to go: weekdays win

![A traditional knife shop front](/images/tours/asakusa-kappabashi-knife-tour/gallery-6.webp)

Kappabashi is a working street, so it keeps business hours. Most shops open from morning until early evening, and a large share close on Sundays and public holidays. A weekday or Saturday daytime visit is the safest plan.

The street is about a 10-minute walk from Asakusa Station, and closer still from Tawaramachi Station on the Tokyo Metro Ginza Line. That makes it an easy add-on to a morning at Senso-ji.

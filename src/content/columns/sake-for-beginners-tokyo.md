If you are new to sake in Tokyo, you only need a handful of words: the type (junmai, ginjo), the temperature (cold or warm), the size (one go is about 180 ml) and one useful question, "Osusume wa nan desu ka?" ("What do you recommend?"). For a first glass, a chilled junmai ginjo is a gentle place to start. Sip slowly and drink water alongside it.

In Japanese, the drink most visitors call sake is usually called *nihonshu* (日本酒). The word *sake* (酒) on its own can mean alcohol in general, which is why a menu may list beer, shochu and nihonshu side by side.

In a small Shinjuku bar, ordering sake can feel like a test. The menu is handwritten, the bottles have no English labels, and the person next to you seems to know exactly what to say. The good news is that bartenders are used to this question, and a few words go a long way.

## ① The menu words: what junmai, ginjo and daiginjo mean

![A tiny bar entrance with a red lantern](/images/tours/goldengai-nightlife/gallery-7.webp)

Japan has a set of official "special designation" grades. Two ideas sit behind them.

**【How much the rice is polished】** Brewers polish away the outer layer of each rice grain. The number on the label, *seimaibuai* (精米歩合), tells you how much of the grain is left. A smaller number means more polishing, and usually a lighter, more fragrant sake.

**【Whether alcohol was added】** *Junmai* (純米) means "pure rice": no distilled alcohol added. Grades without the word junmai may have a small amount added, which can make them lighter and crisper.

| On the menu | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Junmai (純米) | Rice, water, koji and yeast only, no added alcohol |
| Honjozo (本醸造) | Rice polished to 70% or less, a little alcohol added |
| Ginjo (吟醸) / Junmai ginjo | Rice polished to 60% or less, often fruity and aromatic |
| Daiginjo (大吟醸) / Junmai daiginjo | Rice polished to 50% or less, the most delicate style |

A higher grade is not automatically "better". It is simply a different style, and many regulars love a simple junmai with food.

## ② Hot, cold or in between: how to say it

Sake can be served across a wide range, from well chilled to hot. These are the words you will hear most:

- **Hiya / reishu (冷や / 冷酒)**: Reishu means chilled. Hiya is tricky. Traditionally it means room temperature, but many people use it for cold sake, so add "tsumetai" (cold) if you want it chilled.
- **Jo-on (常温)**: room temperature.
- **Nurukan (ぬる燗)**: gently warmed, around body temperature or a little above.
- **Atsukan (熱燗)**: hot, often given as around 50°C.

**【Which one first?】** Ginjo and daiginjo are usually served chilled so their aroma comes through. Warm sake is a comfort in winter and suits fuller, simpler styles. If you are unsure, cold is the safer choice.

## ③ Cups, carafes and sizes

![A neon-lit alley in Golden Gai](/images/tours/goldengai-nightlife/gallery-4.webp)

**【The vessels】**

- **Tokkuri (徳利)**: a small carafe, used for both warm and cold sake.
- **Ochoko (お猪口)**: the small cup you pour into from the tokkuri.
- **Masu (枡)**: a square wooden box. Some bars place a glass in a masu and pour until it overflows. This is a casual, generous style, not a formal ritual: sip from the glass first, then tip the extra in or drink from the box.
- Many bars today simply serve sake in a wine glass or a small tumbler.

**【The sizes】** Sake is traditionally measured in *go* (合). One go, *ichi-go* (一合), is about 180 ml, and *ni-go* (二合) is double. One go shared between two people fills several small cups. A "glass" on a menu may be smaller than a full go, so look for the volume next to the price.

## ④ Dry or sweet? Asking for a recommendation

The two flavour words to know are **karakuchi (辛口)**, dry, and **amakuchi (甘口)**, sweet. Some menus also show a number called *nihonshu-do* (日本酒度), the Sake Meter Value: as a rough guide, plus numbers lean dry and minus numbers lean sweet. Acidity changes the feel too, so treat it as a hint, not a rule.

**【Words that do the work】**

1. **"Nihonshu, onegai shimasu."** (日本酒、お願いします) Sake, please.
2. **"Osusume wa nan desu ka?"** (おすすめは何ですか?) What do you recommend?
3. **"Karakuchi / amakuchi ga suki desu."** (辛口 / 甘口が好きです) I like dry / sweet.
4. **"Tsumetai no / atsukan de."** (冷たいの / 熱燗で) Cold / hot, please.
5. **"Ichi-go de."** (一合で) One go, please.

**【Two more styles you may see】** *Nigori* (にごり) is cloudy sake, coarsely filtered so some rice solids remain. It is often, but not always, on the sweet side. *Namazake* (生酒) is unpasteurised, fresh and lively, and kept refrigerated.

## ⑤ Drinking manners: pour for others, sip slowly

![Guests laughing with the Ninja guide in a back alley](/images/tours/goldengai-nightlife/gallery-2.webp)

**【Pouring】** Traditionally you pour for your companions and let them pour for you, rather than filling your own cup. Hold the tokkuri with one hand and support it with the other. When someone pours for you, lift your cup and hold it with both hands. In casual bars people are relaxed about this, but the gesture is always appreciated.

**【The toast】** Wait until everyone has a drink, then say **"Kanpai!"** (乾杯) before your first sip.

**【Water between sips】** Sake lovers have a word for the water you drink alongside sake: *yawaragi-mizu* (和らぎ水), roughly "softening water". A sip of water between cups refreshes your palate and helps you keep a clear head. Ask for **"O-mizu, onegai shimasu"** (お水、お願いします), water please.

**【Age and pace】** The legal drinking age in Japan is 20, and it applies to visitors too, so carry your passport. Small cups go down easily, so order one go at a time, eat something, and stop when you have had enough. Nobody will mind.

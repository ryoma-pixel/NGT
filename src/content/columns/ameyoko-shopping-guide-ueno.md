Ameyoko is Ueno's open-air market street, running about 400 metres along the train tracks from Ueno Station to Okachimachi Station, with hundreds of small shops selling snacks, seafood, dried foods, fruit, cosmetics and bargain clothes. Go for the cheap snacks and street food, stay for the noise, and come on a weekday morning if you want room to move.

Step out of the station and the first thing you notice is the sound. Shopkeepers call out prices, trains rattle overhead, and a man behind a counter piled with chocolate is shouting to anyone who slows down. Fruit glows under bare bulbs, fish sit on ice, and the smell of grilled meat drifts out from somewhere under the tracks.

It can feel like a lot at first. Here is how to read the street, what is worth buying, and how to time your visit.

## ① What Ameyoko is: a post-war market that never slowed down

![Fruit stalls along Ameyoko](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/gallery-1.webp)

**【Where does the name come from?】** Ameyoko is short for Ameya Yokocho, roughly "candy shop alley". The street took shape in the hard years right after World War II, when food was scarce and black markets grew up around big stations. Many of the early sellers dealt in sweets, and the name stuck.

**【What is it like today?】** Tourism guides count somewhere between 400 and 500 shops along the street and the lanes beside it. You will find:

- Seafood and dried foods (the street's traditional strength)
- Snack and sweet shops selling in bulk
- Fruit stalls and street food counters
- Cosmetics, sneakers, bags and bargain fashion
- Imported foods from across Asia

Part of the market sits under the elevated railway, so a fair amount of it stays dry when it rains.

## ② The chocolate auction: Ameyoko's best free show

![A vendor holding up snacks at a Tataki-uri street auction](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/story-2.webp)

**【What is tataki-uri?】** *Tataki-uri* means "bang-selling": a fast, loud sales pitch where the seller bangs a box or calls out non-stop while adding goods to the pile. Ameyoko is one of the few places in Tokyo where you can still see it.

**【How does it work?】** The best-known stall is Shimura Shoten, which has been running its chocolate sale for decades. The seller fills a bag with chocolates and snacks one after another, shouting as he goes, and adds a "bonus" at the end. You raise your hand, pay, and take the bag. Guides describe the price as ¥1,000 a round for a bag worth well over that, but check the sign on the day.

**【Tips for joining in】**

- Have the exact note ready so the exchange is quick.
- You do not need Japanese. A raised hand is enough.
- Just watching is fine too, and it is a good way to feel the street's energy.

## ③ What to eat as you walk

![A seafood stall in Ameyoko](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/gallery-7.webp)

**【Street food to look for】**

- Cut fruit in a cup or on a stick, sold by the fruit grocers
- Kebabs from stands run by Turkish and South Asian traders
- Seafood bowls and fresh fish dishes at small counters
- Grilled skewers and snacks at the bars under the tracks

**【Can I eat while walking?】** In most of Tokyo, people finish their food at the stall rather than eating on the move. Busy market lanes like Ameyoko are a common exception, but many stalls still have a counter or a spot to stand. If there is one, eat there, and keep your rubbish until you find a bin or the shop takes it back.

## ④ Go underground: the Ameyoko Center Building basement

Most visitors walk straight past the Ameyoko Center Building. Head down to the basement and you find a small food market of shops selling ingredients from China, Korea, Southeast Asia and South Asia: spices, sauces, instant noodles, canned goods, fresh vegetables, meat and fish.

**【Who is it for?】** Home cooks living in Tokyo, travellers looking for ingredients they miss from home, and anyone curious about a side of Tokyo food culture that is far from sushi and ramen. Listings give the hours as 10am to 8pm with Wednesdays closed; check before you go.

## ⑤ What to buy: a quick shopping list

| If you want | Look for |
| --- | --- |
| Gifts for friends | Bulk Japanese snacks and sweets, chocolate from the auction |
| Something to eat now | Cut fruit, kebabs, seafood bowls, skewers |
| Food souvenirs | Dried seafood, tea, dried foods |
| Cooking ingredients | Spices and Asian groceries in the Center Building basement |
| Bargain shopping | Sneakers, bags, casual clothes, cosmetics |

**【Can I bargain?】** Ameyoko is one of the few places in Tokyo where a little polite haggling is accepted at some stalls, and fresh food is often cheaper towards the end of the day. Keep it light and friendly. If the answer is no, smile and either buy or move on.

## ⑥ When to go: timing, cash and crowds

![Ameyoko at night](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/gallery-3.webp)

**【Opening hours】** Each shop sets its own hours, but most open around 10am and close in the early evening, roughly 7pm to 8pm. Some shops close on Wednesdays. After the shops shut, the street turns into a night-time strip of bars and izakaya.

**【Crowds】**

- Weekday mornings are the calmest time to browse.
- Weekends are packed, so expect to move slowly.
- Late December is the busiest time of the year. People from all over Tokyo come to buy seafood and other food for the New Year, and the street can be so crowded it is hard to walk.

**【Money】** Bring cash. Some shops accept cards or IC cards like Suica, but coins and small notes make snacks and auction purchases much easier.

**【Weather】** Much of the market is outdoors. In the hot, humid months of summer, an early start helps.

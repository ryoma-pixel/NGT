If you are not Japanese, your name is normally written in katakana, the script Japan uses for foreign words and names. There is no official kanji version of a foreign name: a kanji name is a creative choice, made by picking characters for their sound, their meaning or both. That makes it a lovely souvenir, especially on a name seal, as long as you check what the characters really say.

Walk through Shinjuku and you will see both ideas on the same street. Katakana spells out borrowed words on shop signs and record sleeves, while some older cafés still write "coffee" in kanji chosen purely for their sound. Your name can live in either world.

Here is how each one works, and how to choose well if you want your name carved in kanji.

## ① Katakana first: your name already has a Japanese spelling

![Shelves of retro Japanese goods](/images/tours/shinjuku-hanko/gallery-6.webp)

**【Why katakana?】** Japanese uses three scripts. Kanji and hiragana write Japanese words, and katakana writes words and names from other languages. Teaching material from the Japan Society in the UK puts it simply: unless you are Japanese, your name will be written in katakana.

**【Is there one correct spelling?】** Not always. Japan's guideline for writing loanwords, a 1991 Cabinet notice that the Agency for Cultural Affairs publishes, sets out which katakana to use, but individual names follow convention. Common names usually have a spelling Japanese readers recognise at once; others have several in use.

## ② Letters out, sounds in: how a name becomes katakana

Katakana spells sounds, not letters. Each character is a syllable, and a few habits cover most names.

**【Consonants get a vowel】** Japanese syllables end in a vowel (or n), so extra vowels appear. Chris becomes クリス (Kurisu) and Smith becomes スミス (Sumisu).

**【Long vowels get a bar】** A long sound is marked with ー (chōonpu), and the official guideline writes long vowels this way in principle. Lee is リー (Rī). A seal registration guide from Ota City in Tokyo uses the example name Clark Thomas Andrew, written クラーク トーマス アンドリュー (Kurāku Tōmasu Andoryū).

**【Sounds Japanese does not have】**

| English sound | Usual katakana habit | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L and R | Both use the ラ row (ra, ri, ru, re, ro) | Lee → リー (Rī), Carl → カール (Kāru) |
| V | The バ row (ba, bi, bu...) or ヴ forms (va, vi, vu...) | David → デビッド (Debiddo) or デイヴィッド (Deividdo) |
| th, as in Smith | Usually the サ row (sa, shi, su...) | Smith → スミス (Sumisu) |
| -er at the end | A long vowel with ー | Peter → ピーター (Pītā) |

**【Why one name can have several spellings】** The guideline offers extra kana such as ヴァ (va) for writing foreign words and names closer to the original sound, but newspapers generally avoid them and write バ (ba) instead. So David appears as デイビッド (Deibiddo), デビッド (Debiddo) and デイヴィッド (Deividdo). All three are variants in real use.

**【Ask, then stick with it】** If your name is unusual, ask a Japanese speaker to say it back to you and write down what they hear. Once you have a version you like, use the same one every time.

## ③ Kanji names: a creative choice, not a translation

![A narrow shopping lane in Shinjuku 3-chome](/images/tours/shinjuku-hanko/gallery-8.webp)

**【Kanji for sound: ateji】** Japanese has a long habit of borrowing kanji just for their sound, called ateji. Coffee is normally コーヒー (kōhī), but it can also be written 珈琲 (kōhī), two characters picked for their sound. Retro coffee shops still use it, like the café in the photo above, where 珈琲 sits beside the shop's katakana name. America was once written 亜米利加 (Amerika), which is why 米 (bei, "rice") still stands for the United States in Japanese news.

**【Two ways to build a kanji name】**

1. **By sound:** one character per syllable. Maria, マリア in katakana, could become 真理亜: 真 (ma, "true"), 理 (ri, "reason") and 亜 (a, "next" or "sub-"), the same character that opens 亜米利加.
2. **By meaning:** pick the Japanese word for what your name means. Lily could become 百合, the word for lily, but Japanese readers will read it as yuri, which is a Japanese name of its own.

**【What Japanese readers expect】** Japanese-learning site Tofugu advises against kanji names for everyday use, and in daily life you will still be called by your katakana name. For a seal, a piece of calligraphy or a keepsake, though, a kanji name is part of the fun.

## ④ Right sound, wrong meaning: the pitfalls

**【One sound, many characters】** Most syllables can be written with many kanji. The sound ma alone can be 真 (true), 麻 (hemp) or 魔 (demon). A converter that only matches sounds cannot tell a blessing from a curse.

**【Unlucky sounds】** Four and nine are considered unlucky in Japan because shi sounds like 死 (death) and ku sounds like 苦 (suffering). Characters like these are best left out of a name, whatever the sound.

**【Each character fine, the pair strange】** Kanji combine into words. In 2019 the singer Ariana Grande had 七輪 tattooed on her palm to mean "7 rings". On their own, 七 (shichi) means seven and 輪 (rin) means ring, but together they read shichirin, a small charcoal grill. Check what your characters say together, not just one by one.

**【Before you ink or carve】** Ask a native speaker to read your name aloud and tell you the first thing it makes them think of. If they hesitate, choose again.

## ⑤ From name to seal: choosing kanji for a hanko

![Rows of hanko seals on display in a specialist shop](/images/tours/shinjuku-hanko/story-2.webp)

A hanko is a personal seal, stamped in red ink where others would sign. For why Japan still stamps instead of signing, see our [guide to hanko culture](/column/make-hanko-souvenir-japan). When you choose characters for one, four questions help:

1. **Sound, meaning or both?** Decide what matters most to you. Many people look for characters that roughly match the sound of their name and also carry a meaning they like.
2. **How many characters?** A seal face is small. Ask the shop how many characters fit at the size you choose and still read clearly.
3. **Which lettering style?** Seal script (tensho) is the traditional style for hanko and looks beautiful, while block script (kaisho) is easier to read.
4. **Kanji or katakana?** A katakana seal keeps your name exactly as Japanese people say it.

**【Souvenir seal or official seal?】** A kanji seal from your trip is a keepsake. Official seals are a separate matter: Ota City in Tokyo, for example, registers a foreign resident's seal in the name, registered alias or katakana name shown on the resident record. If you live in Japan and need a seal for paperwork, ask your city office or bank before you order.

Shinjuku Ni-chome (also written Nichome or 2-chome) is Tokyo's best-known LGBTQ+ neighbourhood: a few blocks of small bars, cafés and clubs just east of Shinjuku Station. First-timers of any identity, allies included, are generally welcome. The key is to come as a guest, not a sightseer: respect who each bar is for, ask before you take photos, and let the community set the tone.

By day, Ni-chome looks like an ordinary corner of Shinjuku, with office workers and convenience stores. Look up, though, and you notice signboards stacked up the sides of older buildings, each one a bar not much bigger than a living room. After dark the signs light up, rainbow flags appear in doorways, and on the main street, Naka-dori, people stand outside with a drink and chat.

## ① What is Ni-chome? A few blocks with a big place in Tokyo life

![A guest taking in the streets of Shinjuku Nichome](/images/tours/shinjuku-nichome-lgbtq/gallery-7.webp)

**【Where it is】** The name simply means the "2nd district" of Shinjuku in Tokyo's address system. The heart of it is a handful of blocks around Naka-dori, within walking distance of Shinjuku-sanchome, Shinjuku-gyoemmae and Shinjuku stations.

**【How many bars?】** Estimates often run to several hundred bars, but the counts differ from guide to guide, so treat any number as a rough idea. What everyone agrees on is the scale: many bars have only a few seats, often up a narrow staircase.

**【Who is it for?】** The district grew up around gay bars for men, but today you will also find lesbian bars, bars for specific communities, mixed bars that welcome everyone, cafés and clubs.

## ② From red-light district to rainbow district

![Bar signs stacked along a Nichome building](/images/tours/shinjuku-nichome-lgbtq/gallery-8.webp)

**【Older than the word "LGBT"】** As nippon.com describes it, Shinjuku's openness to all kinds of people goes back to the huge black markets that grew around the station after the Second World War. Diversity here was never limited to one group.

**【1958: a turning point】** Ni-chome was once a red-light district. Japan's Prostitution Prevention Law, passed in 1956 and in force from April 1958, closed those businesses and left behind many small, empty premises.

**【The bars move in】** Accounts differ on the exact dates. Some trace gay bars in Ni-chome back to the 1950s. Time Out Tokyo says gay venues first gathered in neighbouring San-chome and reached Ni-chome in the second half of the 1960s, naming New Sazae, opened in 1966, as one of the first. Over the following decades, Ni-chome became the centre of Tokyo's gay scene, and later of its lesbian scene too.

## ③ What to expect on your first night

![Guests looking around a Nichome street hung with colorful lanterns](/images/tours/shinjuku-nichome-lgbtq/gallery-6.webp)![A colorful café entrance in Shinjuku Nichome](/images/tours/shinjuku-nichome-lgbtq/story-2.webp)

**【Small rooms, real conversations】** A typical Ni-chome bar is a counter and a few stools. You talk with the bartender and whoever is sitting next to you. That closeness is the point, and it is why each bar has its own regulars and personality.

**【Every bar has its crowd】** Some bars are mainly for men, some for women, some hold women-only nights, and some focus on a particular scene. Mixed bars, often called "mix bars", welcome people of any gender or sexuality and make an easy first stop. You may also see "members only" signs. One nightlife guide notes this does not always mean "no foreigners", but it does signal a room for regulars. If a sign or the staff tells you a bar is for a different crowd, thank them and try the next one.

**【Cover charges and cash】** Many small bars add a cover or seat charge, sometimes with a drink included. Amounts vary, so check at the door before you sit down. Our guide to [cover charges and otoshi](/column/japanese-bar-cover-charge-otoshi) explains how this works in Japan. Guides also note that many Ni-chome bars take cash only.

**【Not only at night】** Cafés and daytime spots let you get a feel for the streets before the bars open.

## ④ Etiquette for first-timers and allies: a guest, not a sightseer

Ni-chome is where people come to relax and be themselves, not a stage set. A few habits go a long way:

1. **Ask before any photo of people.** Never photograph people on the street or inside a bar without clear permission, and expect some bars to ban photos altogether. Japan's national tourism organisation also reminds visitors that permission to take a photo is not permission to post it.
2. **Don't guess or ask about identities.** Nobody owes you an explanation of who they are. Talk about the music, the bar or your trip, and let people share what they want to share.
3. **Order a drink and join in.** Each bar is someone's business and someone's night out. Standing in the doorway to look is not the same as visiting.
4. **Allies: follow the room's lead.** As one local nightlife guide puts it, most Ni-chome bars are LGBTQ+ spaces first that also welcome allies. Going with LGBTQ+ friends, or starting at a mixed bar, is a good way in.

## ⑤ Getting there, timing and a relaxed night

![Warm lights in a narrow Shinjuku alley](/images/tours/shinjuku-nichome-lgbtq/gallery-3.webp)

**【Getting there】** The nearest station is Shinjuku-sanchome (Tokyo Metro Marunouchi and Fukutoshin lines, Toei Shinjuku Line). From JR Shinjuku Station it is about a 10-minute walk.

**【When to go】** Ni-chome gets going in the evening and is busiest late on Friday and Saturday nights. A weekday evening is quieter, and often easier for a first conversation at the counter. Opening hours differ from bar to bar and many open late, so check each bar's website or social media before you go.

**【A relaxed night】** Travel guides describe Ni-chome itself as relaxed. The usual nightlife habits still apply: choose bars yourself rather than following anyone from the street, keep an eye on your glass, and check your last train home. The legal drinking age in Japan is 20.

## ⑥ Beyond the bars: community, Pride and Tokyo today

**【A community centre】** Ni-chome is also home to akta, a non-profit community centre that has worked in the area since 2003. It shares HIV/AIDS information and hosts exhibitions and gatherings open to anyone, whatever their sexuality. Check its current opening times before you visit.

**【Pride】** Tokyo's biggest Pride event, organised by the non-profit Tokyo Rainbow Pride, is not held in Ni-chome. In 2026 the festival took place at Yoyogi Park in June, with a parade through Shibuya and Harajuku. Check the official site, pride.tokyo, for next year's dates.

**【Tokyo today】** Since November 2022, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government has run a Partnership Oath System that gives same-sex couples an official certificate. It is not the same as marriage, and what life is like for LGBTQ+ people in Japan today is one of the topics covered on our [Ni-chome walk](/tour/shinjuku-nichome-lgbtq).

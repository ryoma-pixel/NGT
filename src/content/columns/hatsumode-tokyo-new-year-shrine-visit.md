Hatsumode is the first visit of the New Year to a Shinto shrine or a Buddhist temple, made to give thanks for the year just ended and to pray for the one ahead. In Tokyo most people go during the first three days of January, and many go just after midnight on New Year's Eve. Visitors of any background are welcome to join in: all you need is a coin, some patience and a warm coat.

On the last night of the year, Tokyo stays out late for something quieter than a party. People queue under paper lanterns, stalls along the approach sell hot drinks and snacks, and at temples a deep bell rings out the old year. When midnight comes, the line starts to move, and everyone takes a turn to bow, pray and make a wish. A day or two later the mood is softer, and the crowds thin a little more each day.

## ① What is hatsumode? A first hello to the new year

**【The custom】** Kokugakuin University's Encyclopedia of Shinto defines hatsumode as a visit to a shrine or temple at the beginning of the year, and, in the narrow sense, the visit on New Year's Day. It traces the custom to toshigomori, the old practice of staying awake all night at your local shrine from the evening of New Year's Eve.

**【Shrine or temple?】** Both count. Shrines (jinja) belong to Shinto, Japan's native faith, and honour the kami; you enter through a torii gate. Temples (tera) are Buddhist. In Asakusa you can see both side by side, as explained in [why there is a shrine next to a temple](/column/sensoji-vs-asakusa-shrine).

## ② When to go: midnight, New Year's Day, or a little later

**【Just after midnight】** According to the same encyclopedia, many people now arrive late on December 31, hear the temple bells ring in the New Year (joya no kane), and make their hatsumode on the spot. Senso-ji says its bell on Bentenyama, a small mound in the temple grounds, is struck 108 times from midnight by members of a bell-ringing group, which takes about 40 minutes.

**【January 1 to 3】** These are the main days. GO TOKYO, the city's official travel guide, notes that hatsumode lasts about three days and that shrines get less crowded as the days pass.

**【After the 3rd】** There is no strict cut-off: your first visit of the year is your hatsumode. Senso-ji, for example, holds New Year prayer services in its main hall until January 7.

## ③ What to expect: lines, stalls, charms and fortunes

![A wall of paper lanterns in an Asakusa street](/images/tours/asakusa-night-photo-walk/gallery-5.webp)

**【Lines】** At the famous places, expect to queue to reach the hall. Meiji Jingu, for example, makes its approach one-way over the New Year period.

**【Food stalls】** Japan's national tourism organization describes rows of yatai (street stalls) outside temples and shrines, selling hot drinks and snacks such as udon and yakisoba. Look out for amazake, a sweet, warm drink made from rice and koji.

**【Out with the old charm】** Many people bring back last year's omamori (charms) and ofuda (paper talismans), then choose new ones. A Japanese government explanation of the return box at Meiji Jingu says the items are burned in a sacred fire, and that the box is not for rubbish. Ideally, return a charm to the place it came from. Asakusa Shrine, for example, says it sets up a return place around the end of the year and accepts old items until mid-January.

**【Fortunes and wishes】** Drawing an omikuji fortune slip is part of the fun: here is [how to draw and read one](/column/how-to-draw-omikuji-fortune). You can also write a wish on an ema, a small wooden plaque.

## ④ How to pray when there is a line behind you

![Guests walking with the Ninja guide at Hanazono Shrine](/images/tours/shinjuku-secret-shrine/gallery-3.webp)

The ritual is the same as on any other day, only quicker. At a shrine: drop a coin in the offering box, bow twice, clap twice, pray silently and bow once more. At a temple such as Senso-ji, press your palms together and pray without clapping. The full step-by-step, including the water basin, is in [how to pray at a Japanese shrine](/column/how-to-pray-japanese-shrine).

- **Keep it short.** Have your coin ready, pray, then step aside so the next person can move up.
- **Photograph the place, not the people praying,** and follow any signs about photos.

## ⑤ Famous or local? Choosing your shrine

City people tend to head for famous shrines, the encyclopedia notes. You don't have to.

| Place | Type | At New Year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meiji Jingu (Harajuku) | Shrine | The busiest in Japan, long lines |
| Senso-ji (Asakusa) | Temple | Very busy, midnight bell, stalls |
| Hanazono Shrine (Shinjuku) | Shrine | Shinjuku's local guardian shrine |
| Asakusa Shrine (Asakusa) | Shrine | Small, next door to Senso-ji |

**【Meiji Jingu】** GO TOKYO calls it the most popular place in Japan for hatsumode, with more than three million visitors over the first three days of the year. In recent seasons it has stayed open through the night of December 31. Its own guidance says the busiest times are usually late on New Year's Eve into the small hours, and from mid-morning on January 1 to 3.

**【Senso-ji】** Tokyo's oldest temple, reached through Kaminarimon and the shopping street of Nakamise. Come for the bell at midnight, or for the New Year prayer services in the main hall.

![The Kaminarimon lantern framed by Nakamise at night](/images/tours/asakusa-night-photo-walk/gallery-3.webp)

**【Hanazono Shrine】** Shinjuku's guardian shrine sits between the department stores of Shinjuku-sanchome and the tiny bars of Golden Gai. It is much smaller than Meiji Jingu, but it is the neighbourhood's own shrine, so expect company around midnight. Its website says kagura (sacred dance) and festival music are performed there from New Year's Eve into New Year's Day. More in our [Hanazono Shrine guide](/column/hanazono-shrine-shinjuku-guide).

**【Asakusa Shrine】** A few steps from Senso-ji's main hall, this small shrine honours the three men linked to the temple's founding: an easy second stop to compare a shrine prayer with a temple one.

## ⑥ Practical tips: timing, trains, cash and the cold

![Kaminarimon’s giant lantern on a rainy night](/images/tours/asakusa-night-photo-walk/gallery-1.webp)

- **Dodge the peak.** Going by Meiji Jingu's guidance, avoid the hours around midnight and the late mornings of January 1 to 3. In general, crowds thin out after the 3rd.
- **Check the trains.** In recent years JR East has run several Tokyo lines, including the Yamanote Line, through the night of December 31, though far less often than usual. Other companies decide for themselves, and not all run all night, so check this year's plans.
- **Bring cash.** You need coins for the offering box, and cash is the safe choice for charms, fortunes and stalls.
- **Dress for standing still.** January is Tokyo's coldest month. Days are often sunny and dry, but midnight lines are cold: wear layers.
- **Stay together.** Agree on a meeting point and follow staff directions in dense crowds.

Hours, routes and train plans change every year, so check the official sites before you go.

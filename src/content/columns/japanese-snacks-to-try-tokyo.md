The Japanese snacks to try in Tokyo come in three groups: crunchy rice crackers like senbei, factory classics such as Pocky and Japan-only Kit Kat flavours, and tiny, cheap dagashi that Japanese kids grow up on. Add one fresh traditional sweet, like dango or dorayaki, and you have tasted the whole range. The easiest place to find them all in one walk is Ameyoko in Ueno.

Walk into a Japanese snack aisle for the first time and it is a little overwhelming. Whole walls of crackers, chocolate sticks in flavours you have never heard of, and rows of tiny packets that cost almost nothing. Most of the packaging is in Japanese, so it is hard to tell what is sweet and what is salty.

This list sorts the classics by type, so you know what you are picking up, and then shows where in Tokyo to buy them.

## ① Rice crackers: the salty side of Japan

![Busy Ameyoko market lanes](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/gallery-2.webp)

**【What are they?】** Japanese rice crackers are older than any chocolate bar on this list. They are thought to have come from China many centuries ago, and today they fill entire shelves in Japanese supermarkets.

**【Three names to know】**

- **Senbei**: large, round and flat, made from ordinary (non-sticky) rice and baked. Soy sauce is the classic flavour, sometimes wrapped in a sheet of seaweed. Some are very hard, so bite carefully.
- **Okaki**: made from sticky mochi rice, cut into small blocks and baked. Crisp outside, a little lighter inside.
- **Arare**: also made from mochi rice, but small. The name means "hailstones", after their shape.

**【How to try them】** Buy a mixed bag. Many include several shapes and flavours, often with peanuts, and they go well with green tea or a cold beer.

## ② Pocky and Kit Kat: the famous factory classics

**【Pocky】** Glico launched Pocky in 1966. The name comes from *pokkin*, the snapping sound of a stick breaking in two. Its clever detail is the bare "handle" at the end, so your fingers stay clean. Beyond the basic chocolate, look for seasonal and regional versions you will not see at home.

**【Kit Kat】** Kit Kat is the big surprise for many visitors. In Japanese, the name sounds like *kitto katsu*, "you will surely win", so students receive it as a good-luck gift before exams. Nestle Japan has released hundreds of limited flavours since 2000, from matcha and sakura to flavours tied to one region. Some are only sold in a certain area or season, so if you see one you like, buy it then.

## ③ Dagashi: Japan's coin-operated childhood

![A vendor holding up snacks at a Tataki-uri street auction](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/story-2.webp)

**【What are dagashi?】** Dagashi are cheap snacks and sweets made for children, the Japanese version of penny candy. The name roughly means "humble sweets". They became popular in the hard years around World War II, when shops selling them spread across Japan's neighbourhoods.

**【What to pick up】**

- **Umaibo**: a puffed corn stick that comes in many sweet and savoury flavours, from cheese to corn soup. It sold for just ¥10 for decades, and its first price rise came in 2022. Check the label for today's price.
- Ramune-flavoured candies that taste like the Japanese lemonade soda
- Milk candies and small caramels
- Fruit gummies and sour chews

**【Why they make good souvenirs】** They are light, colourful and cheap, so you can bring back a bag of twenty different ones for friends without filling your suitcase.

## ④ Fresh sweets: dango, dorayaki and wagashi

![A traditional Japanese sweet shop](/images/tours/shinjuku-gyoen-sakura/gallery-2.webp)![Choosing sweets at a shop counter](/images/tours/shinjuku-gyoen-sakura/gallery-3.webp)

Not every snack comes in a packet. Traditional sweets, called *wagashi*, are made fresh at small shops and are best eaten the same day.

**【Three to look for】**

- **Dango**: chewy rice dumplings on a skewer, often glazed in sweet soy sauce or topped with sweet bean paste.
- **Dorayaki**: two soft pancakes with sweet red bean paste in the middle. In Ueno, Usagiya, founded in 1913, is one of the best-known makers in Tokyo. Listings say it closes on Wednesdays; check before you go.
- **Seasonal sweets**: in spring, look for sakura mochi wrapped in a pickled cherry leaf.

**【Good to know】** Sweet red bean paste (*anko*) appears in many Japanese sweets. If you are not sure you will like it, buy one piece first.

## ⑤ Where to buy: a quick map of Tokyo snack shopping

| Where | Best for |
| --- | --- |
| Ameyoko, Ueno | Bulk snacks at discount prices, dagashi, the snack auction |
| Convenience stores | Seasonal Pocky and Kit Kat, quick single packs |
| Supermarkets | Rice crackers and everyday snacks at local prices |
| Tokyo Okashi Land, Tokyo Station | Shops run by big snack makers, freshly made items and limited editions |
| Small wagashi shops | Fresh dango, dorayaki and seasonal sweets |

**【Ameyoko's snack shops】** Niki no Kashi has been on Ameyoko since 1947 and is known for a huge range, often said to be around 5,000 items, with many priced below regular shops. It runs two stores facing each other across the street. A few steps away, the snack auction stalls fill a bag with chocolates while the seller shouts, which is half snack shopping and half street show.

**【Tokyo Okashi Land】** In the First Avenue shopping street at Tokyo Station, by the Yaesu side, makers such as Glico, Morinaga, Calbee and Kameda Seika run their own shops, some with items you can only buy there. It is a good last stop before a Shinkansen or an airport train.

## ⑥ Snack etiquette and tips

- **Eating while walking**: in most of Tokyo, people eat at the shop or stand to one side rather than walking with food. Busy market streets are more relaxed, but if a stall has a counter, use it.
- **Bins are rare**: carry a small bag for wrappers until you find a bin.
- **Cash helps**: card payment is common, but small stalls and auctions are easier with coins and notes.
- **Check the date**: fresh wagashi does not keep. Packaged snacks travel better for gifts.

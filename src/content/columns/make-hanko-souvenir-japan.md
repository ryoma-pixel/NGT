In Japan, when buying a house, opening a bank account, or even just receiving a package, people take out their "personal stamp" instead of signing their name with a pen.

This stamp is called a **"Hanko"** or "Inkan." Why is this traditional stamp still being used today, even as digitalization advances?

Today, we will introduce the secret of Japan's fascinating Hanko culture and the charm of making your own Hanko, which is currently booming among foreign tourists!

### What is a Hanko?

![](/images/columns/make-hanko-souvenir-japan/01.webp)

A Hanko is a small, usually cylindrical stamp engraved with a person's name (typically their family name). It is pressed onto paper using a special red ink called *Shuniku*.

It carries the exact same legal weight as a signature in Western countries. In Japan, it is common for a single adult to use several different Hanko depending on the purpose (such as an officially registered seal for contracts, a bank seal, and an everyday seal).

### Why Use a Hanko Instead of a Signature?

The reason for using a Hanko instead of a handwritten signature is deeply connected to Japanese history and spirituality.

- **The Weight of Responsibility and Determination**

  You can quickly write a signature in just a few seconds. However, the act of pressing a Hanko involves specific steps: "applying the red ink," "aligning the position on the paper," and "pressing down with force." Taking this mindful pause carries a cultural significance of showing responsibility and seriousness toward one's decision.
- **Identity and Artistry**

  The calligraphy used for Hanko characters is incredibly beautiful, and no two are exactly alike. It is not just a tool; it is a work of art that can be called an "alter ego" of the person who owns it.

### Take Japanese Culture Home! "Original Hanko"

![](/images/columns/make-hanko-souvenir-japan/02.webp)

Currently, a rapidly growing number of foreign travelers are captivated by this Hanko culture and are making their own "original Hanko" to take home as a souvenir of their trip to Japan!

The experience of creating a Japanese name by matching your first name to *Kanji* characters with similar sounds, and then having a professional artisan carve it—or even carving the stone yourself—is truly one of a kind.

Because you can stamp it as your "Japanese-style signature" on letters, artwork, and notebooks after returning home, it is incredibly popular as a practical and supremely cool souvenir.

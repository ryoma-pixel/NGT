In many Japanese izakaya and small bars you pay a per-person seat charge, and at izakaya it usually arrives as a small dish you did not order, called *otoshi*. It is a normal custom, not a scam. The simple fix is to ask about the charge before you sit down, then relax and enjoy the dish.

Picture the scene. You squeeze onto a stool under a paper lantern, order a beer, and before it even arrives a tiny bowl appears in front of you: a few pieces of simmered vegetables, some edamame, perhaps a spoonful of potato salad. Nobody asked what you wanted. At the end of the night, there it is on the bill.

For many first-time visitors this is the most confusing moment of a night out in Tokyo. Here is what is going on, and how to handle it like a regular.

## ① What otoshi is: a seat charge that comes with a snack

**【What kind of dish is it?】** Otoshi is a small plate served soon after you are seated, one per person. It is usually something simple from the kitchen that day. Travel guides often mention edamame, simmered dishes, a small salad or pickles, and at casual places even something like popcorn. Many otoshi are meat-free, but some include fish or meat, so it is not a reliable vegetarian option.

**【What are you really paying for?】** Most explanations agree that otoshi works as a table or seat charge. You are paying for the seat and the service, and the dish is the restaurant's way of saying thank you for coming in. In western Japan the same thing is often called *tsukidashi*.

**【Why do places charge it?】** Two reasons come up again and again:

- Guests often stay for a long time over a few drinks, and the charge helps cover the table.
- Japan does not have a tipping culture, so the seat charge is one of the ways a small place keeps going.

## ② Bars, izakaya and the "charge" sign

![A tiny bar entrance with a red lantern](/images/tours/goldengai-nightlife/gallery-7.webp)

The word you will see most often is simply "charge", written in English or as チャージ. It means the same idea: a fee per person for your seat.

| Where | What it is usually called | What you get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Izakaya (Japanese pub) | Otoshi, or tsukidashi in western Japan | A small dish with your first drink |
| Small bars, such as in Golden Gai | Charge, cover charge, seat charge | Your seat, sometimes a snack |
| Standing bars (*tachinomi*) | Usually none | Nothing extra, you just stand |
| Upscale or hotel restaurants | Service charge | A percentage added to the bill |

**【Golden Gai and other tiny bars】** In alleys of small bars, such as Shinjuku's Golden Gai, many bars charge a cover fee on top of your drinks. Some post it on the door, but not all of them do. Bars that welcome visitors often have English signs, and a few post signs saying they are for regulars only. Respect those and move on to the next door.

**【Service charge is something else】** At expensive restaurants and hotels you may see a service charge added as a percentage of the bill. That is separate from otoshi and is normally mentioned on the menu.

## ③ How much is it? Check before you sit down

The amount depends on the place. At a casual izakaya it is usually a few hundred yen per person; small bars and upscale places can charge more. Because prices change, the most reliable figure is the one on the sign at the door or the one the staff tell you.

![A neon-lit alley in Golden Gai](/images/tours/goldengai-nightlife/gallery-4.webp)

**【Three phrases that help】**

1. *Otōshi wa arimasu ka?* (Is there an otoshi?)
2. *Chāji wa ikura desu ka?* (How much is the charge?)
3. *Okaikei onegaishimasu.* (The bill, please.)

Asking is not rude. Staff are used to the question, and asking at the start avoids any surprise at the end.

**【On the bill】** The charge may appear as お通し, "otoshi", "table charge" or "seat charge". Some places add the charge even when no dish is served.

## ④ Can you say no? Politely, and only at the start

Some guides say you can decline the otoshi when it is first placed on your table. In practice this is rare in Japan, it can feel awkward, and many places will still add the seat charge because it pays for the table, not the food.

**【Allergies and diets】** This is the one situation where speaking up matters. Tell the staff about allergies or dietary needs as soon as you sit down, before the dish arrives, and ask what the otoshi contains.

**【If it does not suit you】** The polite move is to ask before you take a seat. If the charge is not what you want, thank them and try another place. Once you have eaten the dish, treat it as part of the bill.

## ⑤ Want to skip the charge? Try a standing bar

![Izakaya seats under the train tracks in Ueno](/images/tours/ueno-ameyoko/story-3.webp)

Standing bars, called *tachinomi* or *tachinomiya*, usually have no table charge because nobody takes a seat. You order a drink and a small plate at the counter, and you can leave after one round. Ueno's streets under the train tracks are a good place to see the mix of izakaya and standing counters side by side.

## ⑥ A normal charge versus a bad bill

A seat charge of a few hundred yen, mentioned when you ask, is part of everyday life in Japan. A bill many times higher than anything you were told is a different thing entirely. The habits that protect you are the same everywhere:

- Choose places yourself instead of following anyone who approaches you on the street.
- Ask about the charge and look at the menu prices before ordering.
- Keep track of what you order.

**【No tip needed】** Japan's national tourism organization says tipping is not common in bars, cafes and restaurants. The cover charge is not a tip, so you do not need to add anything on top.
